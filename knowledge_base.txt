# SeedGuide Comprehensive Gardening Knowledge Base

## SECTION: Tomato Growing Guide
Tomatoes are one of the most popular and rewarding garden vegetables. They come in thousands of varieties including cherry, grape, beefsteak, roma, and heirloom types.

### Starting from Seed
Start tomato seeds indoors 6-8 weeks before your last frost date. Plant seeds 1/4 inch deep in seed starting mix. Keep soil temperature between 65-85°F for best germination. Use a heat mat under seed trays for consistent warmth. Seeds typically germinate in 5-10 days.

### Transplanting
Harden off seedlings by gradually exposing them to outdoor conditions over 7-10 days. Transplant after all danger of frost has passed. Bury the stem deep — tomatoes grow roots from any buried stem portion. Space plants 24-36 inches apart.

### Care and Maintenance
Water consistently, providing 1-2 inches per week. Water at the base of the plant, not overhead, to prevent foliar diseases like blight. Mulch heavily to retain moisture and prevent soil splash. Stake or cage indeterminate varieties. Prune suckers (shoots growing in leaf axils) for larger fruit on indeterminate types.

### Common Problems
Blossom end rot is caused by inconsistent watering and calcium deficiency. Hornworms are large green caterpillars — hand-pick them. Early blight causes brown spots on lower leaves — remove affected foliage. Cracking happens from uneven watering.

### Harvesting
Harvest when fruits are fully colored and slightly soft to the touch. Tomatoes continue to ripen after picking. Store at room temperature, never in the refrigerator (kills flavor).

## SECTION: Lettuce and Salad Greens
Lettuce is a cool-season crop that's perfect for beginners. Types include leaf lettuce, romaine, butterhead, and crisphead (iceberg).

### Growing Conditions
Lettuce prefers cool temperatures between 45-75°F. It bolts (goes to seed) in hot weather, becoming bitter. Plant in spring and fall for best results. Partial shade is fine — lettuce needs only 3-6 hours of sun.

### Planting
Sow seeds directly 1/4 inch deep, or start indoors. Seeds need light to germinate — don't bury too deep. Thin seedlings to 6-12 inches apart depending on variety. Succession plant every 2 weeks for continuous harvest.

### Harvesting
For leaf lettuce, harvest outer leaves when 4-6 inches tall (cut-and-come-again method). For head types, wait until a firm head forms. Morning harvest produces the crispest leaves.

## SECTION: Basil and Culinary Herbs
Basil is a warm-weather annual herb essential in Italian, Thai, and many other cuisines. Varieties include sweet basil, Thai basil, purple basil, lemon basil, and holy basil.

### Growing Basil
Start seeds indoors 6 weeks before last frost. Seeds need light to germinate — press into soil surface. Transplant after frost danger, or grow on a sunny windowsill year-round. Needs full sun (6-8 hours) and warm temperatures above 60°F.

### Harvesting and Maintenance
Pinch off flower buds as they appear to keep the plant producing leaves. Harvest from the top, cutting just above a leaf pair to encourage branching. Regular harvesting creates a bushier plant.

### Other Essential Herbs
- **Cilantro**: Cool-season herb that bolts quickly in heat. Succession plant every 3 weeks. Seeds (coriander) are also edible.
- **Parsley**: Biennial herb, very cold hardy. Italian flat-leaf has more flavor than curly types.
- **Rosemary**: Perennial woody herb. Needs excellent drainage. Can be grown in containers and brought indoors in winter.
- **Thyme**: Low-growing perennial. Very drought tolerant once established. Needs well-draining soil.
- **Oregano**: Hardy perennial. Spreads aggressively — consider containers. Flavor is strongest just before flowering.
- **Chives**: Hardy perennial with mild onion flavor. Beautiful purple flowers are edible. Divide every 3 years.
- **Dill**: Annual herb that attracts beneficial insects. Self-seeds readily. Great companion for cucumbers.
- **Sage**: Perennial shrub. Drought tolerant once established. Harvest before flowering for best flavor.
- **Lavender**: Technically an herb. Needs excellent drainage and full sun. Drought tolerant.

## SECTION: Peppers - Sweet and Hot
Peppers include sweet varieties (bell, banana, pimento) and hot varieties (jalapeño, habanero, cayenne, ghost pepper). All are warm-weather crops.

### Starting Peppers
Start seeds indoors 8-10 weeks before last frost. Peppers need warm soil (70-85°F) for germination — use a heat mat. Germination takes 7-14 days. Very slow growing initially.

### Growing Requirements
Full sun, warm temperatures. Wait until nighttime temps stay above 55°F to transplant. Space 18-24 inches apart. Peppers are lighter feeders than tomatoes. Too much nitrogen produces leaves, not fruit.

### Hot Pepper Special Notes
Capsaicin (heat compound) increases with plant stress — slightly less water produces hotter peppers. Wear gloves when handling very hot varieties. Scoville scale measures heat: jalapeño (2,500-8,000), habanero (100,000-350,000), ghost pepper (1,000,000+).

## SECTION: Cucumbers and Melons
Cucumbers are prolific warm-season crops. Types include slicing, pickling, and burpless varieties.

### Growing Cucumbers
Direct sow after frost, or start indoors 3 weeks before transplanting. Grow vertically on a trellis to save space and prevent disease. Water consistently — inconsistent watering causes bitter fruit. Harvest frequently to keep plants producing.

### Watermelon
Watermelons need a long, warm growing season (80-100 days). Plant in hills with 6-8 feet spacing. Need lots of water during fruit development. Ripe when the tendril nearest the fruit turns brown and the ground spot turns yellow.

### Cantaloupe/Muskmelon
Similar to watermelon but slightly shorter season (75-90 days). Ripe when the stem slips easily from the fruit and the melon is fragrant. Needs warm soil and full sun.

## SECTION: Root Vegetables
Root vegetables include carrots, beets, turnips, radishes, and potatoes.

### Carrots
Must be direct sown — don't transplant. Need loose, rock-free soil for straight roots. Germination is slow (14-21 days) — keep soil surface moist. Mix seeds with sand for even sowing. Frost improves flavor.

### Beets
Easy to grow. Each "seed" is actually a cluster of seeds — thin carefully. Both roots and greens are edible. Ready in 55-70 days. Cold tolerant.

### Radishes
Fastest vegetable to grow — ready in 22-30 days. Direct sow, thin to 1-2 inches. Don't leave too long in ground — they become woody. Plant as row markers for slow-germinating crops.

### Potatoes
Plant seed potatoes (not grocery store potatoes) 4 inches deep. Hill soil around plants as they grow. Harvest new potatoes when plants flower, or wait for full-size when tops die back. Cure in cool, dark place.

### Sweet Potatoes
Need long, warm growing season (90-120 days). Plant slips (shoots from sweet potatoes), not seeds. Need loose soil and warm temperatures. Cure after harvest for sweetness.

### Turnips
Cool-season root vegetable. Both roots and greens are edible. Ready in 40-60 days. Very cold tolerant. Direct sow in spring or fall.

### Onions
Can be grown from seed, sets, or transplants. Long-day varieties for northern areas, short-day for southern. Need loose soil and consistent moisture. Ready when tops fall over.

### Garlic
Plant individual cloves in fall for summer harvest. Plant pointed end up, 2 inches deep. Mulch heavily. Hardneck varieties produce scapes (flower stalks) — cut and eat them. Cure in warm, dry location after harvest.

## SECTION: Leafy Greens
Beyond lettuce, there are many nutritious leafy greens to grow.

### Spinach
Cool-season crop that bolts quickly in heat. Very cold hardy — can survive light freezes. High in iron and vitamins. Soak seeds overnight for faster germination. Succession plant every 2-3 weeks.

### Kale
Extremely cold hardy — frost improves flavor. Can be harvested through winter in many climates. Harvest outer leaves, leaving center to continue growing. Varieties: curly, lacinato (dinosaur), red Russian.

### Swiss Chard
Beautiful and productive. Heat tolerant unlike spinach. Harvest outer stalks. Colorful varieties (Rainbow, Bright Lights) add beauty to the garden. Can produce for several months.

### Arugula
Peppery salad green. Very fast growing (ready in 21-40 days). Bolts in heat — best in cool weather. Self-seeds readily.

### Collard Greens
Heat and cold tolerant — one of the most versatile greens. Traditional southern cooking staple. Large, sturdy leaves. Harvest from bottom up.

### Mustard Greens
Spicy flavored greens. Very fast growing. Cool-season crop. Many varieties from mild to very spicy.

## SECTION: Beans and Peas
Legumes are nitrogen-fixing crops that improve soil health.

### Green Beans
Bush types produce all at once; pole types produce over a longer period. Direct sow after frost — don't transplant. Don't soak seeds before planting (can crack them). Pick frequently to keep producing.

### Peas
Cool-season crop — plant as early as soil can be worked. Types: shelling peas, snap peas, snow peas. Provide support for climbing. Inoculate with rhizobium for better nitrogen fixation. Harvest frequently.

### Lima Beans
Need longer, warmer season than green beans. Direct sow when soil is warm (65°F+). Bush varieties are easier for containers.

## SECTION: Squash Family (Cucurbits)
Squash comes in summer and winter varieties.

### Zucchini/Summer Squash
Incredibly productive — one plant can feed a family. Harvest small (6-8 inches) for best flavor. Check daily once fruiting starts. Male flowers appear first. Watch for squash vine borer.

### Winter Squash
Includes butternut, acorn, spaghetti squash, pumpkins. Need long growing season (80-120 days). Can be stored for months after curing. Need lots of space.

### Pumpkins
Same family as squash. Need 75-120 days depending on variety. Giant varieties need extra feeding and watering. Cure in sun for 10 days after harvest.

---

## SECTION: Corn
Sweet corn needs space and proper pollination. Plant in blocks (at least 4 rows) rather than single rows for wind pollination. Direct sow after soil is warm (60°F+). Ears are ready when silk turns brown and a kernel squeezed produces milky liquid. Use immediately — sugar converts to starch quickly after harvest.

---

## SECTION: Brassicas (Cole Crops)
The brassica family includes broccoli, cauliflower, cabbage, Brussels sprouts, and kohlrabi.

### Broccoli
Cool-season crop. Start indoors 6-8 weeks before transplanting. Harvest central head when buds are tight. Side shoots will continue producing. Rich in vitamins and minerals.

### Cauliflower
Most challenging brassica. Needs consistent cool temperatures. Blanch white varieties by tying leaves over the head. Ready when head is firm and compact.

### Cabbage
Very cold hardy. Green and red varieties. Can be stored for months. Ready when head is firm. Spring and fall plantings.

### Brussels Sprouts
Need long, cool growing season (80-100 days). Harvest from bottom up as sprouts firm up. Frost improves flavor dramatically. Remove lower leaves as you harvest.

---

## SECTION: Fruiting Plants
### Strawberries
Three types: June-bearing (one large crop), everbearing (2-3 crops), day-neutral (continuous). Best from transplants, not seed. Runners create new plants. Mulch with straw. Net against birds.

### Blueberries
Need acidic soil (pH 4.5-5.5). Plant at least 2 varieties for cross-pollination. Take 2-3 years to produce well. Amend soil with peat moss and sulfur. Net against birds.

### Raspberries
Very productive once established. Spread by runners — contain in raised beds or with root barriers. Summer-bearing fruit on second-year canes; everbearing fruit on first-year canes.

### Blackberries
Similar to raspberries but more heat tolerant. Thornless varieties available. Very productive. Train on trellis.

## SECTION: Companion Planting Guide
Companion planting is the practice of growing certain plants together for mutual benefit.

### Classic Companions
- **Three Sisters**: Corn, beans, and squash planted together. Corn provides structure for beans, beans fix nitrogen, squash shades soil.
- **Tomato + Basil**: Basil may repel tomato pests and improve flavor.
- **Carrots + Onions**: Each repels the other's pest flies.
- **Lettuce + Tall plants**: Tall plants provide shade for heat-sensitive lettuce.
- **Beans + Potatoes**: Beans fix nitrogen that potatoes need; potatoes repel Mexican bean beetle.
- **Marigolds**: Plant throughout the garden to repel nematodes and many pests.
- **Nasturtiums**: Trap crop for aphids. Edible flowers.

### Plants to Keep Apart
- Tomatoes and brassicas (broccoli, cabbage) — compete for nutrients
- Beans and onions/garlic — onions inhibit bean growth
- Dill and carrots — can cross-pollinate
- Fennel and most vegetables — fennel inhibits growth of nearby plants

## SECTION: Pest Management
### Common Garden Pests
- **Aphids**: Tiny soft-bodied insects on new growth. Spray with strong water jet. Attract ladybugs. Neem oil spray.
- **Tomato Hornworm**: Large green caterpillar on tomato plants. Hand-pick. Leave them if they have white parasitic wasp cocoons attached.
- **Squash Vine Borer**: Moth larva that enters squash stems. Wrap stem base with foil. Inject Bt into affected stems.
- **Cucumber Beetle**: Yellow-green striped or spotted beetle. Spreads bacterial wilt. Row covers until flowering. Trap crops.
- **Japanese Beetle**: Metallic copper-green beetle. Hand-pick into soapy water. Milky spore for lawn grubs. Neem oil.
- **Cabbage Worm**: Green caterpillar on brassicas. Bt spray is effective. Row covers prevent moths from laying eggs.
- **Slugs/Snails**: Night feeders. Beer traps, copper barriers, diatomaceous earth. Hand-pick at night.
- **Spider Mites**: Tiny mites on undersides of leaves. Spray with water. Neem oil. Increase humidity.
- **Whiteflies**: Tiny white flying insects. Yellow sticky traps. Neem oil. Reflective mulch.
- **Flea Beetles**: Tiny jumping beetles that create shothole damage. Row covers. Neem oil. Trap crops.

### Organic Pest Control Methods
- **Neem Oil**: Effective against many soft-bodied insects. Apply in evening to avoid burning leaves.
- **Bt (Bacillus thuringiensis)**: Biological control for caterpillars. Safe for beneficial insects.
- **Diatomaceous Earth**: Powder that damages insect exoskeletons. Apply when dry. Food-grade only.
- **Insecticidal Soap**: Effective against soft-bodied insects. Make your own: 1 tbsp castile soap per quart water.
- **Row Covers**: Physical barrier against pests. Remove when plants need pollination.
- **Companion Planting**: Marigolds, nasturtiums, and herbs can deter pests.
- **Beneficial Insects**: Encourage ladybugs, lacewings, parasitic wasps, praying mantis.

## SECTION: Disease Prevention and Management
### Common Plant Diseases
- **Powdery Mildew**: White powder on leaves. Improve air circulation. Baking soda spray (1 tbsp per gallon + few drops soap).
- **Early Blight**: Brown concentric rings on tomato/potato leaves. Remove affected foliage. Mulch to prevent soil splash.
- **Late Blight**: Devastating disease of tomatoes and potatoes. Remove and destroy affected plants immediately.
- **Damping Off**: Seedlings collapse at soil line. Use sterile soil mix. Don't overwater. Improve air circulation.
- **Fusarium/Verticillium Wilt**: Soil-borne fungi. Plant resistant varieties (look for V, F on seed packets). Rotate crops.
- **Blossom End Rot**: Dark, sunken area on tomato/pepper bottom. Not a disease — caused by calcium deficiency and inconsistent watering.

### Prevention Strategies
- Rotate crops annually (don't plant same family in same spot)
- Water at the base of plants, not overhead
- Mulch to prevent soil splash onto leaves
- Remove diseased plant material immediately
- Choose disease-resistant varieties
- Ensure proper spacing for air circulation

## SECTION: Soil Health and Composting
### Understanding Soil
Good garden soil has a balance of sand, silt, and clay (loam). It should drain well but retain moisture. Healthy soil is full of microorganisms and earthworms.

### Soil Testing
Test soil pH and nutrients every 2-3 years. Most vegetables prefer pH 6.0-7.0. Contact your local extension service for affordable soil testing. Adjust pH with lime (raise) or sulfur (lower).

### Composting Basics
Compost is decomposed organic matter — the best soil amendment. Balance green materials (nitrogen-rich: kitchen scraps, fresh grass) with brown materials (carbon-rich: dry leaves, cardboard). Turn regularly for faster decomposition. Ready when it's dark, crumbly, and smells earthy.

### What to Compost
- **Green (Nitrogen)**: Fruit/vegetable scraps, coffee grounds, fresh grass clippings, plant trimmings
- **Brown (Carbon)**: Dry leaves, straw, cardboard, newspaper, wood chips, sawdust
- **DO NOT compost**: Meat, dairy, diseased plants, pet waste, treated wood

### Worm Composting (Vermicomposting)
Red wiggler worms break down kitchen scraps into nutrient-rich castings. Perfect for apartments — can be done in a bin under the sink. Worms eat their weight in food daily.

## SECTION: Watering Guide
### General Principles
- Most vegetables need 1-2 inches of water per week
- Water deeply and less frequently rather than shallow and often
- Morning watering is best — leaves dry quickly, reducing disease
- Water at the base, not overhead
- Mulch retains moisture and reduces watering needs by 25-50%

### Signs of Watering Issues
- **Underwatered**: Wilting, dry soil, crispy leaf edges, blossom drop
- **Overwatered**: Yellow leaves, soggy soil, root rot, fungus gnats
- **Inconsistent**: Cracking (tomatoes), bitter fruit (cucumbers), blossom end rot

### Container Watering
Containers dry out faster than garden beds. May need daily watering in summer. Use self-watering containers or drip systems. Stick finger 1 inch into soil — water if dry.

### Irrigation Methods
- **Hand watering**: Good for small gardens. Time-consuming but allows you to inspect plants.
- **Soaker hoses**: Efficient, deliver water at soil level. Good for rows.
- **Drip irrigation**: Most efficient method. Delivers water directly to roots. Can be automated with timer.
- **Sprinklers**: Least efficient. Wets foliage (disease risk). Useful for lawns.

## SECTION: Seed Starting Indoors
### Equipment Needed
- Seed starting trays or small pots
- Seed starting mix (NOT regular potting soil — too dense)
- Heat mat (for warm-season crops)
- Grow light or sunny south-facing window
- Spray bottle for gentle watering
- Labels and permanent marker

### Timing
Count backwards from your last frost date. Each seed packet tells you when to start. Common timing:
- 8-10 weeks before: peppers, eggplant
- 6-8 weeks before: tomatoes, basil
- 4-6 weeks before: squash, cucumbers, melons (if starting indoors)
- 2-4 weeks before: lettuce, herbs

### Hardening Off
Gradually expose indoor seedlings to outdoor conditions over 7-10 days before transplanting. Start with 1-2 hours of outdoor shade, gradually increase time and sun exposure. Bring in at night if frost is possible.

## SECTION: Container Gardening
Container gardening is perfect for apartments, balconies, and small spaces.

### Container Selection
- Bigger is almost always better — more soil holds more water and nutrients
- Ensure drainage holes in the bottom
- Dark containers heat up faster (good in cool climates, bad in hot)
- Self-watering containers reduce maintenance

### Best Plants for Containers
- **Small containers (1-2 gallon)**: Herbs, lettuce, radishes, spinach
- **Medium containers (3-5 gallon)**: Peppers, bush beans, strawberries, chard
- **Large containers (5+ gallon)**: Tomatoes, cucumbers, zucchini, eggplant

### Container Growing Tips
- Use quality potting mix (not garden soil)
- Containers need more frequent watering and feeding than garden beds
- Fertilize regularly since nutrients wash out with watering
- Group containers to create a microclimate and retain humidity
- Elevate containers for drainage and to prevent root rot

## SECTION: Raised Bed Gardening
### Benefits
- Better drainage and soil control
- Warmer soil in spring (earlier planting)
- Reduced bending and easier access
- Clear boundaries prevent compaction
- Can be placed over poor soil or concrete

### Construction
- Common sizes: 4x4, 4x8 feet (reach to center from sides)
- Height: 6-12 inches minimum, 24+ for accessibility
- Materials: untreated cedar, redwood, concrete block, galvanized steel
- AVOID: pressure-treated wood (chemicals leach into soil), old railroad ties

### Filling
Fill with a mix of topsoil, compost, and peat moss/coconut coir. The classic mix is 1/3 each. Soil will settle — top off annually with compost.

## SECTION: Seasonal Gardening Calendar

### Spring (March-May)
- Start warm-season seeds indoors (tomatoes, peppers)
- Direct sow cool-season crops (peas, lettuce, spinach, radishes)
- Prepare beds — add compost, test soil
- Last frost date is key — know yours!
- Set up trellises and supports

### Summer (June-August)
- Transplant warm-season crops after last frost
- Keep up with watering — daily for containers
- Mulch heavily to retain moisture
- Succession plant quick crops (beans, lettuce)
- Watch for pests and diseases
- Harvest regularly to keep plants producing

### Fall (September-November)
- Plant cool-season crops for fall harvest
- Plant garlic for next summer
- Clean up spent summer crops
- Add compost to beds
- Protect tender plants from early frost with row covers
- Plant cover crops in empty beds

### Winter (December-February)
- Plan next year's garden
- Order seeds
- Start perennial seeds that need cold stratification
- Maintain tools
- Build or repair raised beds
- Read gardening books!

## SECTION: Organic Gardening Principles
### What is Organic Gardening?
Organic gardening avoids synthetic fertilizers, pesticides, and herbicides. It focuses on building healthy soil, encouraging beneficial organisms, and working with nature.

### Key Practices
- Build soil with compost, cover crops, and organic matter
- Rotate crops to prevent disease buildup
- Encourage biodiversity — plant flowers to attract pollinators and beneficial insects
- Use physical barriers (row covers, netting) before chemical controls
- When pest control is needed, use organic-approved products (neem oil, Bt, diatomaceous earth)
- Conserve water with mulch and efficient irrigation

### Benefits
- No synthetic chemical exposure for you or your family
- Healthier soil ecosystem
- Better for pollinators and beneficial insects
- More sustainable long-term
- Many people report better flavor from organically grown produce

## SECTION: Preserving Your Harvest

### Canning
- Water bath canning for high-acid foods (tomatoes, pickles, jams)
- Pressure canning for low-acid foods (green beans, corn, meat)
- Always follow tested recipes from USDA or Ball/Kerr

### Freezing
- Blanch vegetables before freezing to preserve color and nutrition
- Spread on tray first (flash freeze), then transfer to bags
- Most vegetables freeze well for 8-12 months

### Drying
- Herbs: hang bunches upside down in warm, dry area
- Use a food dehydrator for fruits and vegetables
- Store dried items in airtight containers

### Fermentation
- Sauerkraut, kimchi, pickles
- Requires only salt, water, and vegetables
- Rich in probiotics
- Ancient preservation method

## SECTION: Troubleshooting Common Problems

### Why won't my seeds germinate?
- Soil too cold or hot for that variety
- Planted too deep
- Soil too wet or dry
- Old seed (check germination rate)
- Some seeds need light to germinate (don't bury)

### Why are my plants yellow?
- Overwatering (most common cause)
- Nitrogen deficiency
- Iron chlorosis (in high pH soil)
- Not enough light
- Root bound (in containers)

### Why no fruit/flowers?
- Too much nitrogen (all leaves, no flowers)
- Not enough sun
- Temperature too hot or cold
- Poor pollination
- Plant too young

### Why are my plants wilting?
- Underwatered (check soil moisture)
- Overwatered (root rot)
- Transplant shock (temporary)
- Disease (check stems for damage)
- Too much heat

### Why are leaves curling?
- Viral infection
- Aphid damage
- Herbicide drift
- Environmental stress (wind, heat)
- Overwatering

## SECTION: Growing Food in Small Spaces

### Balcony Gardening
- Use railing planters and hanging baskets
- Choose compact/dwarf varieties
- Vertical growing (pole beans, tomatoes on trellis)
- Consider wind exposure — may need wind breaks
- Weight limits — check with building management

### Windowsill Gardening
- Best for herbs: basil, chives, mint, parsley
- South-facing window gets most light
- Supplement with grow light if needed
- Small containers (4-6 inch pots)
- Great year-round growing option

### Indoor Growing with Grow Lights
- LED grow lights are most efficient
- Keep lights 2-6 inches from seedlings
- 14-16 hours of light per day for most vegetables
- Timer is essential
- Can grow lettuce, herbs, and microgreens entirely indoors

### Microgreens
- Grow on windowsill in shallow trays
- Ready to eat in 7-14 days
- Incredibly nutritious (up to 40x nutrients of mature plants)
- Popular varieties: sunflower, pea shoots, radish, broccoli
- Need minimal equipment: tray, soil, seeds, light

## SECTION: Eggplant
Eggplant is a warm-weather crop related to tomatoes and peppers. Varieties include classic purple, white, Japanese, and Thai types.

### Growing Requirements
Start seeds indoors 8-10 weeks before last frost. Needs very warm soil (70°F+) for transplanting. Full sun, rich soil, consistent moisture. Space 18-24 inches apart.

### Harvesting
Harvest when skin is glossy and fruit is firm. Overripe eggplant has dull skin and brown seeds inside. Cut (don't pull) from plant.

---

## SECTION: Okra
Okra is a heat-loving crop popular in southern cooking. Very productive in hot weather.

### Growing
Direct sow after soil is warm (65°F+). Soak seeds overnight for faster germination. Space 12-18 inches apart. Can grow 4-6 feet tall.

### Harvesting
Harvest pods when 2-4 inches long — larger pods become tough and woody. Pick every 1-2 days. Wear gloves — okra plants can be prickly.

---

## SECTION: Asparagus
Asparagus is a perennial vegetable that produces for 15-20 years once established.

### Planting
Plant crowns (roots) in trenches 8-12 inches deep. Fill gradually as plants grow. Don't harvest for the first 2 years — let plants establish. Full sun, well-draining soil.

### Harvesting
Harvest spears when 6-8 inches tall by snapping at soil level. Harvest for 6-8 weeks in spring, then let ferns grow to feed roots for next year.

---

## SECTION: Celery
Celery is challenging to grow but very rewarding. Needs a long, cool growing season.

### Growing
Start seeds indoors 10-12 weeks before transplanting. Very slow germination. Needs rich, consistently moist soil. Heavy feeder — fertilize regularly. Blanch stalks by mounding soil or wrapping with paper to reduce bitterness.

---

## SECTION: Leeks
Leeks are a mild, sweet member of the onion family.

### Growing
Start seeds indoors 8-10 weeks before transplanting. Transplant into trenches and fill soil around stems as they grow (blanching). Ready in 80-120 days. Very cold hardy.

---

## SECTION: Artichokes
Globe artichokes are perennial in mild climates, grown as annuals elsewhere.

### Growing
Start seeds indoors 8-10 weeks before last frost. Need vernalization (cold period) to produce buds. Large plants — space 4-6 feet apart. Rich soil, consistent moisture.

### Harvesting
Harvest buds when they're tight and firm, before they open. Cut with 1-2 inches of stem.

## SECTION: Growing Herbs Indoors Year-Round
Many herbs grow well indoors with adequate light:
- **Best for indoors**: Basil, chives, mint, parsley, cilantro, oregano, thyme
- **Need strong light**: Rosemary, sage, lavender
- **Grow light requirements**: 12-16 hours daily
- **Temperature**: Most herbs prefer 60-70°F
- **Humidity**: Group plants together, mist regularly, or use a pebble tray
- **Containers**: 4-6 inch pots with drainage
- **Soil**: Well-draining potting mix
- **Watering**: Allow top inch of soil to dry between waterings
- **Fertilize**: Monthly with diluted liquid fertilizer

## SECTION: Edible Flowers
Many flowers are both beautiful and edible:
- **Nasturtiums**: Peppery flavor. Easy to grow. Trap crop for aphids.
- **Calendula**: Mild, slightly tangy. Also medicinal. Easy to grow.
- **Violets**: Sweet flavor. Hardy. Shade tolerant.
- **Borage**: Cucumber flavor. Attracts pollinators. Self-seeds.
- **Sunflowers**: Seeds are edible. Easy to grow. Attract birds.
- **Pansies**: Mild, slightly minty. Cool-season. Beautiful garnish.
- **Lavender**: Floral flavor. Use sparingly. Great in baking.
- **Chamomile**: Apple-like flavor. Makes excellent tea. Easy to grow.
- **Marigolds (Gem types)**: Citrusy flavor. Also repel pests.

## SECTION: Cover Crops and Green Manure
Cover crops are planted to improve soil rather than for harvest.

### Benefits
- Prevent erosion
- Add organic matter
- Fix nitrogen (legumes)
- Suppress weeds
- Improve soil structure

### Common Cover Crops
- **Crimson Clover**: Fixes nitrogen, beautiful flowers, attracts pollinators
- **Winter Rye**: Cold hardy, suppresses weeds, adds organic matter

## SECTION: Garden Planning and Design
### Square Foot Gardening
Method that divides raised beds into 1-foot squares. Each square planted with specific number of plants based on size. Maximizes production in small space. Great for beginners.

### Succession Planting
Planting the same crop every 2-3 weeks for continuous harvest. Works great for lettuce, radishes, beans, and other quick-maturing crops.

### Crop Rotation
Rotate plant families through different beds each year:
- Year 1: Nightshades (tomatoes, peppers) → Year 2: Legumes (beans, peas) → Year 3: Brassicas (broccoli, cabbage) → Year 4: Root crops (carrots, beets)
- Prevents disease buildup in soil
- Balances nutrient demands

### Interplanting
Growing fast and slow crops together. Example: radishes between rows of carrots. Radishes are harvested before carrots need the space.
